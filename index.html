Challenging the Call:
MLB's ABS Revolution

For 150 years, one human being stood 60 feet, 6 inches from a 95-mph fastball and decided the fate of at-bats. In 2026, teams got the power to appeal that decision to a machine. The data reveals how badly they needed it — and how hard the job really is.

The Strike Zone You Think You Know

The rulebook says a strike is any pitch that passes through the zone — a rectangle extending from the batter's knees to the midpoint of their torso, and from the inner to outer edges of home plate. Simple enough. But the zone that actually gets called is a different shape entirely.

The pattern is impossible to miss. Umpires squeeze the top of the zone and give away the bottom. They shade toward the catcher's glove, widen for certain counts, and shrink the zone for elite pitchers. These aren't random errors — they're systematic, predictable biases that batters and catchers could now, for the first time, appeal directly.

Across 69,000 pitches in the 2026 season, umpires disagreed with the ABS geometric zone on roughly 9.5% of called pitches — not a lot, but in a game decided by fractions of a run, every one of those calls matters.

But Not Every Team Got the Memo

Here's where the and-but-therefore of ABS gets interesting. The system gives every team the same resource: two challenges per game. Succeed, and you keep the challenge. Fail, and it's gone. By the end of May, the gap between the best and worst challenge teams had become striking.

Texas leads the league at over 63% — meaning nearly two out of every three challenges they make are overturned in their favor. Milwaukee sits last, winning fewer than one in three. That gap represents a genuine strategic skill that is being learned and exploited in real time across the league.

The New Skill: Knowing When to Challenge

Challenging well isn't just about identifying bad calls — it's about reading pitch location in the fraction of a second after the ball crosses the plate, estimating how close to the edge the pitch was, and deciding whether the probability of a successful challenge outweighs the cost of losing one. It is, in other words, a statistical decision made instinctively.

Every Overturned Call Changes the Game

An overturned strike becomes a ball. An overturned ball becomes a strike. Those aren't abstract statistics — they change counts, extend at-bats, and, in the most consequential cases, flip strikeouts into walks and walks into strikeouts. At the team level, ABS is already acting as a second front in the competitive arms race.

What ABS Has Revealed

The single most important insight from the first months of ABS is one that no one fully anticipated: the difficulty of calling balls and strikes isn't a flaw in human performance — it's a property of the problem itself. A pitch on the corner moves 6 inches in the last 10 feet of flight. Perspective distortion from behind the plate makes the inside corner look different from the outside corner. And the zone itself is a moving target, shifting inch by inch with every batter's stance.

Even with the machine available for appeals, teams are only converting roughly half their challenges. Even experienced catchers, who have spent entire careers reading pitch location, are getting it wrong nearly as often as right on the hardest pitches. The human umpire was not failing at an easy task. They were succeeding at an incredibly hard one.

ABS didn't expose umpires as incompetent. It exposed the strike zone as the most contested two-square-foot rectangle in professional sports — and gave every batter, pitcher, and fan a front-row seat to just how close every call really is.
